CChristlicher SoldatSep 05 2008, 05:35 PM
What precisely is the assumed value of a united world? If you mean that the world ought to be united under one goverment (patriotic unity), then you face the problem of civil, ideological disunity. Patriotic unity never equates to ideological unity. Patriotism is harnessed by Democrats and Republicans alike -- the one saying that being "progressive" is "the American way" and the other saying that commitment to our national origins is "the American way." Though both are united in the patriotic sense, they define differently the ideological grounds for that unity, and so though they are one federal nation, the United States are probably more divided than they have ever been since the American Civil War. The only way to achieve ideological unity ends up being the Soviet way: silence all diversity of opinion. Thus begins the blind spiral of consequentialist ethics. We will justify violence against the people, whether physical or intellectual violence, on the grounds that it will pave the way to a better tomorrow.If you mean that the world ought to be united under one philosophy of coexistence, independent of a patriotic unity, then you face the problem of a self-refuting worldview. Postmodernism and the literature of "unity at the expense of the exclusivity of truth" certainly appeals to the emotions, but it's incoherent. It accuses exclusivist worldviews (in this case, nationalism) of being the source of all evil, and yet it itself is an exclusivist worldview because all whoits enemies are labeled "intolerant," "bigoted," and "fanatical" and are thus excluded. Then it engages in an ignorant sort of special pleading, saying that its own standards of truth don't apply to it. "Obviously," it says, "the verbiage of coexistence can't be nationalism." But if I want to retain my exclusivist truth claims, then I don't get to be in its club. Essentially -- if this is what you mean by your statement -- you end up adopting a nationalism that rejects all other nationalism, which is the same view you are trying to refute.And what of this talk of "manipulation"? Are you not trying to influence us with your opinion? You will protest that this is not manipulation, that this is not comparable to using propaganda to foster in me certain positive emotions for my country. Yet you select words that will make your position come across as appealing, as a position that you are justified to hold and that I would be justified in agreeing with. Words that will elicit in me certain positive responses toward your opinion. This is fundamental to any act of persuasion.My own position, the orthodox Reformed and Lutheran Christian position--Two Kingdom theology--evades these logical self-contradictions. In a nutshell, the governing authority of my country ought to be obeyed and respected so long as his demands do no damage to my moral conscience. And yet it is never my place to use the state in such a way as to violate the moral consciences of others, except where the necessity of domestic tranquility (keeping the general peace) is concerned. I think this has several distinct advantages. On the one hand, this system of thought avoids the Church-State dominionism of the "Religious Right" and historic Roman Catholicism, but on the other hand it avoids the antinomian, civil-disobedience-as-a-virtue of anabaptists and socialists. Moreover, the whole issue of how we ought to feel about our governments is acknowledged for the side issue that it really is. I am patriotic (or, as you might say, "nationalist"), but not for its own sake. Rather, patriotism flows from more general moral principles. Unity is a means to civil peace, but not the end. Manipulation is rejected not because it itself is evil, but because it is harnessed toward evil ends. As such, our opinion about patriotism does not emerge as a first principle. We have an objective moral basis already prepared in the Christian gospel, and we simply apply it to generate secondary principles about national loyalty.
