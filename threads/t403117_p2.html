I am under the impression that most posters here have never dealt personally with suicide. I see a lot misconceptions being posted here and I would like to take some time to clear it up. Many of the sentiments displayed here are deeply hurtful to remaining family members and don't really reflect actual suicide at all. First and foremost, suicide is the result of depression which is a serious illness. Depression is a medical condition. A depressed person can no more control their suicidal thoughts than a drunk person can control the slurring of speech. Both are indicative that some hormone or chemical in the brain is off balance. I first came to understand suicide in high school. At that time my mother began talking about how horrible life was and started suggesting that suicide was looking more and more like an escape. Such discussions made me pretty uncomfortable but I dismissed it in my mind as verbal hyperbole. I didn't even know what to do even if I did suspect an actual attempt so I just ignored it. Luckily, one of her superiors at her office had noticed and sent her to get an evaluation. Doing so they probably saved her life and in the long wrong improved her life ten fold. Depression had always been prevalent in my family. As a teenager my mother already exhibited the symptoms that characterize teenage depression. Not long afterwards, she was raped by a family member who then threatened to kill her. In the years afterward, she suffered serious relationship issues with men and was unable to feel happy. Finally my youngest brother was born with a severe mental retardation and she found little support from her husband. Her teenage depression had never been resolved and was compounded by all the trauma and stress she experienced. Imagine the worst day of your life. Now imagine feeling like that for 30 years and what seems like no way out. That was the situation my mother was in. It is easy for everybody else to dismiss suicide as cowardly because they have no concept of the degree of misery. Luckily she was pressured to find treatment. In doing so, it was discovered that the levels of serotonin in her brain was very imbalanced. In order to solve the problem of her depression all she had needed was a controlled dose of serotonin. With a single treatment, she returned to managing her emotions just as any other person would and entertained no more thoughts of suicide and felt fulfillment in life again. Three years later my other brother began exhibiting signs of depression. His doctor convinced him to go on medication and what followed was a marked improvement in his behavior. Even though nothing else in their stressful lives has changed, both feel much happier and no longer entertain thoughts of suicide because their serotonin levels are now properly managed. Someone who is depressed cannot just "get over it" or "fix the problems." Something internal has been knocked off balance and requires either medication or intense therapy. A depressed person cannot recognize their emotions for a serious medical condition. All they know is that they are feeling immense emotional pain. These feelings have been present for so long in the lives of depressed individuals that they believe it is their normal state. Only someone on the outside can recognize depression for what it is and encourage treatment for it. The stigmatization of depression and other mental illness only discourages people from the treatment they need. It falls to the family members to seek help. However, depression and suicide is not easy to recognize for what it is or how to address it. A family member could have saved a relative who committed suicide but they couldn't recognize the severity of the situation of how to solve it. A family member is left feeling guilt because we could have done something, we just didn't know.The other case for suicide is to control one's own fate. This is the case for a terminal illness or life altering illness. At advanced stages suffering from cancer is equivalent to suffering from torture for months on end. The pain that is felt from cancer or a comparable illness cannot even be managed by morphine. That is how severe the pain is. Is it really so terrible that a patient who will die anyway has to suffer torment while they're at it? Most people don't even put their dogs and cats through that but humans should be made to endure it? And for what reason? Because we bystanders who don't have cancer cells eating up our insides want them to be superheroes. I have seen someone in their last moments of life suffering from cancer and let me tell you that there is NOTHING cowardly or selfish about committing suicide. At the end of her life this patient's body was covered in sores from the cancer. She was barely aware of even her closest family members and struggling to draw breath against the pain. This is NORMAL for cancer. Why is it objectionable for them to die by suicide in a painless, more dignified, and less traumatizing manner? Calmly accepting that the end is coming. The other case for suicide is in the event of degrading mental faculties. I could not bear to inflict upon my love ones, a life where I must live with Alzheimer's. Not only must they anticipate utter humiliation and degradation but also the increasing frustration and resentment of their family members. Is it unreasonable of these individuals to want to be remembered fondly and with respect by their family members and not as an invalid? Speaking from the experience of a relative of a child with severe mental retardation, you can feel love for someone under your care and intense resentment at the same time.
