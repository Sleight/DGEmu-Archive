WWolf O'DonnellAug 22 2010, 06:32 PM
That's a terrible idea.Let me start practical objections before I go on to ethical objections.In using adult human beings, you have limited what you can do. You could test new surgical techniques and do toxicology tests. However, you cannot do drug tests in order to show that a drug works. Researchers currently use gene knock-out animals to do that. To create a gene KO animal, you have to get embryonic stem cells, get rid of the genes in question, implant those embryonic stem cells into a blastocyst and then implant that into a womb. Wait until the mother gives birth, then grab those animals who have some of modified cells and breed them together. Continue until you have bred out the normal cells completely and you get a pure gene KO animal where all the cells lack the gene.You can't do that to prisoners. They may be scum of the Earth, but does that mean their children are as well?Now, as for those conditions that don't require genetic modification... what are you going to do? Disable a prisoner in some way? Infect them with a disease?Instantly, we come up with a problem. If it's a nasty disease, it makes far more sense to do it on an animal because there's less chance of that disease escaping and then infecting human beings. For example, AIDS. AIDS vaccines are currently being tested on apes with a simian variant of AIDS that doesn't infect us (or at least, not as well). With human test subjects, it's the actual disease. If that escapes, you've got a big biohazard problem on your hands. (Haven't you seen the horror movies?)And you definitely can't do the LD50 test on prisoners. (Not that it's a good idea doing it on animals either). The toxicology test known as LD50 is cruel and unusual, as it involves finding the dose that kills of half of your test population. That's horrible. Besides which, there can't be that many prisoners in your nation of choice.Oh and that brings me on to another subject. Currently, career guinea pigs are screwing up Clinical Trials in the US. They're going from one trial to another, sometimes without a proper cool-off period to make sure that the drug from teh previous trial has been flushed out of their body. In prisons, this wouldn't so much of an issue, because you would know which prisoner has been in which test. However, you are advocating their use as replacement for animal testing, which would inevitably mean the part of drug development that occurs before Phase 1 Clinical Trials. In Phase 1, the drug has been proven to do what it's supposed to do. It has also been proven to not be too toxic. Phase 1 and subsequent phases are to make sure that the animals aren't giving us false positives or negatives. jtbias, the phase you're advocating is highly experimental. As such, researchers are required to euthanise the animal test subjects afterwards (not to mention that "autopsy" provides data as well).Instantly, this fact means you can only do testing on death row inmates. This drastically decreases the number of test subjects you can effectively experiment on.Here's another issue related to that. By law, (well, UK law at least) the welfare of animals is required to be high at all times. You are supposed to feed the test subject well, ensure it isn't stressed and teat it well. The key is to make sure it isn't stressed, because stressed animals react differently to non-stressed animals. Practially, the animal must represent the average human being, as much as possible. They must be reasonably healthy with the exception of what they're suffering. If they're very stressed, this may mean their physiology is not representative of the drug's intented patient. Now, the one thing you can guarantee about a death row inmate is that he's stressed. This makes him absolutely useless, unless the condition your candidate drug is supposed to treat creates as much stress in the patient as a death row inmate. Instantly, you've reduced the number of conditions you can realistically test for.Now you may argue that the animal test subjects are aware of their fate. I can argue opposite. The point is, I cannot argue the opposite where human beings are concerned. They know. And here's the thing. You will need a placebo, to make sure there's no placebo effect, because they know they're being tested on a drug and that can invoke the placebo test. If you're doing a toxicology test, they might anticipate that the thing you're administering will do them harm, in which case, the opposite nocebo effect will kick in (i.e. they imagine they're going to get worse, so they do). Either way, you now need to use a placebo/nocebo pill, thus increasing the costs of testing.That reminds me. That's not all the increased costs. You think pharmaceutical companies want to do animal testing? It's costly and it doesn't always give the results they want. Given the chance, they'd gladly drop it. Now think of what would happen if you switch to humans. You need to pay for their food, which will be a lot more than animal feed. You also need to pay to give larger doses to them, because ultimately, they will need larger doses than the animal test subjects on account of them being larger than the animal test subjects.To create a novel drug, the pharmaceutical industry already spends about $800 million. That's just for one drug. (And that's an out-of-date 2003 figure). It's going to cost a lot more.Here's another issue. Ultimately, it means the tax payer will be shouldering a part of the costs for developing a drug that the pharmaceutical indstury will profit from. Effectively, what this proposal is advocating is tax-paid subsidies to pharmaceutical industries. Is it right that we as tax payers should help make a private company richer? Or will our share of the burden mean that the government will require the pharmaceutical industry to charge less for the drug, thus meaning less profit for the pharmaceutical company? Either scenario will be unpalatable. The former to Conservatives and the latter to the companies. So neither party would want that to happen, so that once again reduces the number of things you can do with convict test subjects.Now, what about pure research? Well, you can't do most of it on death row inmates. You can't do genetics, because ultimately, that requires breeding them as I've mentioned before. You could do behavioural tests on them, but then again, that's not what you have in mind, is it?I suppose you could test weapons on them, but what if it doesn't work the way you think it does? You then have to pay to euthanise them if it doesn't go the way you want it to. Ultimately, you can't use non-death row inmates even with non-lethal weapons, because you might end up maiming them. Then you'd have to euthanise them, which would be an extra cost to the tax payer. Would you pay extra money to have someone killed, when they weren't supposed to die at all?And I'm sure there's plenty more practical issues that I haven't thought up of yet.Ultimately, though, it's unethical. Yes, animal testing could be considered to be somewhat unethical, but you could potentially argue that animals aren't as aware as we are. You can't make the same arguments with humans, though. With animals, it's likely to be unethical but it might not be. With humans, it definitely is not ethical. And yes, I'm going to invoke Godwin's law here. We are civilised and democratic. (I presume you're from a democratic country). We are not Nazis. We do not experiment on human beings, because we think they are subhuman or evil. What is the difference between us and the Nazis if we were to adopt this idea of experimenting on other human beings, because we consider them to be inferior (and in some cases, Nazis genuinely believed that Jews were evil)?I mean, what with the Nazis rounding all Jews, gypsies, homosexuals and so forth, they're bound to have experimented on someone who was guilty of some crime; some Jew who was genuinely evil. But do you want to say that not all victims of the Holocaust were innocent? And even if it that statement was true, does that make the Holocaust less evil than it would have been if all the victims were innocent?Now here is the second part of my ethical conundrum to hopefully overcome my last statement's shortcomings as an invocation of Godwin's Law. We are presuming that everyone who is in prison is guilty. Not all people in prison are guilty. There are those who are falsely imprisoned and there are those who were falsely executed. Is it justifiable to experiment on these people, knowing that some of them do not deserve it? If we can argue that the Holocaust isn't as bad as we thought, because (statistically speaking at least) some of the people who were gassed were bound to have been guilty enough to deserve it, then surely we can argue that the death penalty and therefore experimenting on prisoners is less justifiable because, statistically speaking at least, some of the people are bound to be innocent and thus undeserving of such cruel punishment.You may argue that the Nazis, however, were wrong to consider Jews evil and inferior. But what if they were right? What if Jews really are inferior and really are evil? Does it make it okay to kill them and experiment on them? I would say, no. Thus, even if someone can prove beyond a doubt that the Nazis were right about Jews being inferior, I can still say that the Jews did not deserve the Holocaust. Not so if we adopt your policy. We would become hypocrites, otherwise. (Interestingly, although this is completely irrelevant to the argument, Hitler was against animal testing too).Now, you may counterargue that the criminals had a choice to do good and evil, just like the Nazis did. Fair enough, but how much of their actions is really down to choice? The majority of prisoners in the US are of African-American descent. If a criminal's actions are really down to choice, why is the population so biased towards black people? If it really was down solely to choice, then we wouldn't see this. The prison population would be representative of the nation as a whole. But it isn't. So what's going on here? Are black people genetically predisposed to crime? Or is it more likely that they're down to being poor? Most prisoners are poor. They come from poor backgrounds. Why is that? Did they choose to be poor? How much of what they did is down to environment?We know that children who are abused are more likely to be abusers themselves. Is it fair to conduct cruel experiments on them because society failed to properly counsel them?Ultimately, no matter who or what you test on, it's not fair; the test subjects don't really deserve it. It's not fair on the animals and it's not fair on the human beings, whether they be prisoners or people taking part in paid Clinical Trials. Is it fair that we predominatly conduct Clinical Trials on career guinea pigs, who feel that because of their situation, being tested on is the best way to earn money? And what if all the Clinical Trials were moved to the Developing World? Is it fair that we test drugs on people who are so poor, they have no choice but to enter clinical trials?
