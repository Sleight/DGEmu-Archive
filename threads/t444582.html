Honestly, I see very few things wrong with her philosophy; I agree that the rights of the individual (which includes freedom, obviously) should be protected above everything else. I feel that everyone should be allowed to do what they want, so long as it does not infringe upon the rights of another, and that for the most part, government should exist solely to make sure these rights are respected. However, I disagree with how she thinks her philosophy should/can be realized. I do not believe that laissez-faire capitalism is a good idea; quality control and monopolies are a few problems that aren't adequately protected against in a laissez-faire system. She argues that the most innovative and inventive people will succeed in such a society, and these innovations push humanity forward. While I can understand why she feels this way, I argue that a lack of regulation could cause disastrous results. Let's say we have two competitors, A and B, with two very very similar products. These products are in fact, so similar that it is difficult to discern any real difference between the two. However, competitor A is somehow able to sell his products at significantly lower prices than competitor B can. Naturally, everyone buys competitor A's product. Unfortunately for them, competitor A took some shortcuts in making his product that cause some sort of grievous (and slow-acting, like cancer, or birth defects in future generations) harm to the consumers. My point is, with proper regulation, competitor A would not be able to harm humanity with their product. Rand's prescription for society disagrees with this.I'm sure Rand's laissez-faire capitalism also allows for sweatshops and child labor to be perfectly okay, and that's totally wrong.
