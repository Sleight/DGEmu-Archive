KKirie MotobaMar 25 2014, 06:18 AM

Seagate drives, it depends which models. 7200.10 models were meant for death: It was the period where they purchased Maxtor and tried all sorts of defunct experiments which lead to drive failures unless you updated the drive firmware.

 

Computer parts need to be balanced, the basics you need to get a working computer:

- Computer case

- Motherboard (do not go cheap on this)

- Processor

- Memory

- Hard drive

- Optical drive

- Power supply (do not go cheap on this)

 

The extras that need to be considered:

- Video card (do not go cheap on this)

- Operating system

- Optional components such as cpu heatsinks, USB 3.0 connectors, etc.

- External peripherals such as printers, keyboards, mouse, etc.

 

Considerations on your budget:

- All items marked as do not go cheap, I mean it. A cheap motherboard: lacking dimm slots preventing future upgrades, bad port design (inserting a video card blocks SATA ports, etc), cheap capacitors (blow out after a bit). A cheap power supply, enjoy the risk of your computer's self-combustion and going up in flames when the PSU dies. A video card, if you're buying one, you're buying one for a reason, don't go cheap and buy a card that you will need to upgrade soon after because it's too crappy.

 

- Choose your tower according to taste, but also according to space and organization. Larger towers = more room to breathe and make sure your motherboard fits inside. Mid ATX, ATX are usually what people aim for. EATX for those that really want a beast, Micro ATX for those that want the smallest things as a case.

- Processor is important to determine what you're doing. Yes, we all know Intel beats the crap out of AMD in nearly all scenarios nowadays, but are you willing to pay that premium price for it? Dual-core, triple-core, quad-core, hexa-core, octo-core, multi-thread virutal cores, all needed? If for gaming only, the max of a quad-core is enough. For intense virtualization of environments, advanced video editing and encoding, and other hardcore stuff, you may need high, but that's a big MAY.

- RAM, standard nowadays is 8GB. You do not need more for gaming unless you're going hardcore again with that extra stuff I mentioned above. There's also different quality of RAM, DDR3 1333MHz, 1600MHz, etc. Latency CAS9-9-9-21, etc. There are variations to everything, choose depending on your needs.

 

Personal recommendations:

- Case: Your choice. Lots of people going Antec, Cooler Master, I also like Fractal Design and NZXT. If you're rich, Lian Li, premium stuff.

- Motherboard: Asus of Gigabyte only. MSI is just dooming your computer for failure, ECS is dooming it even further. Intel boards, you're just asking for trouble. Intel may be very good at their CPUs, but they SUCK ASS at mobos.

- Processor: Intel Core i5-3570k, best bang for the buck on Intel at this generation. No point in going Haswell (i5-4XXX series). AMD FX-8350 for the current gen on AMD processors.

- RAM: G.Skill, best bang for the buck. Corsair, Mushkin, nice second up runners.

- Hard disk: Western Digital all the way for regular drives. Seagate and Toshiba are timebombs that will die out after its expected life a lot of times. If you go SSD, Samsung all the way (extremely fast), then Intel (expensive yes, but extremely reliable).

- Optical drive: Samsung all the way. Pioneer used to be ultra good, but they're producing less and less. Lite-On second up. Avoid LG, they're worthless scrap.

- Power supply: If you're rich: Seasonic all the way (best performance, reliability, endurance, constant power). Best runner up: Corsair, XFX, Cooler Master, Antec. Avoid any generic bullcrap, those do not give you proper voltage during load, thus risking short circuits and burning of your computer components.

- Video card: GTX 660 or 670 are really high end, you'll be running those for years to come. AMD R9 270 are great. Previous gen, AMD HD7870 were awesome also. As for branding, Asus, Gigabyte, Sapphire, all good!

 

Don't forget, if you want to stay legit with Windows, a license of Windows 7 Home Premium or Windows 8.1 are around 100$ each. You're always welcome to crack it though.

KKirie MotobaMar 25 2014, 02:55 PM

There are no easy way to check.

 

The most important usually is:

- Choosing your motherboard based on the socket used for your CPU. If you take an LGA1155 processor, just take a board that supports it.

- When picking the board, note its size (ATX, etc). Then choose a case for it. Also, note if your case supports USB3 ports in the front, and if your board has USB3 front panel connectors.

- When picking the board, note its DIMM slots and RAM type (commonly DDR3 now, dual channel, triple channel in some circumstances). Choose the RAM that goes with it. Remember that on a basis, DDR3 is clocked at 1600MHz with a small tweak in the bios. Any RAM faster than that might need an overclock of our CPU depending on what CPU you choose.

- When picking your board, note how many PCI-E 16x, PCI-E 1x, and PCI slots you have. PCI-E 16x are used for video cards, so if you want crossfire or SLI setups, check out if you want to go 2 way to 3 way, meaning you'll need as many ports. PCI-E 1x is for extension cards, such as sound cards, network adapters, SATA adapters, etc. PCI, same thing, but the older stuff like 56k modem, etc.

- When picking the boards, check how many SATA2 and SATA3 ports you have. Choose your hard disks accordingly.

- Depending on how many hard disks and videos cards you choose, make sure you choose a power supply that has enough molex / SATA / PCI-E power connectors. And for the sake of cable management, and if you have some spare cash to spend on this power supply, go modular. You will not get annoyed trying to shove and tie unused power cables on the side.
