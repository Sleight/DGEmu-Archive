CCalvin LutherJan 25 2013, 01:39 AM

My argument is that horror, at it's most distilled form, is any work that exposes the baser nature of man. It's usually done through an exterior force that represents evil, like monsters or whatever, that threatens to destroy the civilized nature of the characters.

 

In Spec-Ops,

 

Walker's desire to be the hero (and the player's desire to assume the role of a bad-ass soldier) drive him to cause more chaos and more death. That's why the genericness of the first four chapters is so important, because it puts him (and the player) in familiar territory. You shoot the bad guy because that's what you're supposed to do.

 

The horrors of war aren't the horror of the game. The horror of the game is how Walker forces himself to always, always be the hero. His rationalizations (the 33rd has gone rogue, the DJ is evil, Konrad has gone bad, the soldiers forced him to use the WP, stealing the water is the right thing to do) allow him to act as the monster while acting as the hero. The horror isn't the picture of the burnt woman who failed to protect her child, it's the fact that the man who did this to her believes that he was right to do it.

 

Unfortunately, like BR is showing us, the impact of the game doesn't always land on the player.
