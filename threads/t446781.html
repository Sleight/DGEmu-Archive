Well, it's obvious the internet is here to stay. So many people use it every day, and in so many ways, from business to leisure, that it's doubtless the internet will only get bigger and more complex. Leaps in technology and communications backbones have lead to new and different ways to utilize the internet, from telecommuting in real time, to streaming full length movies, to cataloging storehouses of information that can be called upon in an instant. There is no doubt that the internet is one of the greatest unifying achievements of the human race and one of the most useful tools we've ever given birth to.The internet has developed so rapidly that it has seemed to take on a life of its own. Indeed, it has its own culture, etiquette, and feel. The internet even has dark, mysterious places, nooks and crannies where all is not as it seems, and even blind alleyways where danger lurks in many forms. The internet has become an unadulterated extension of us, it is a digital replica of the human condition, and offers something for everyone. Excuse me for being needlessly poetic, but the internet is very much the street market of the human soul.Which brings me to my question:Is the internet the last realm where one can be truly free or is it an insidious breeding ground of lawlessness, hate, and moral corruption? Is it both? I have my own feelings about the internet, which might be hinted at in my opening statements, but I would like to see the community weigh in on this matter before I truly address my opinion. Also, as a bonus, I would like to see what people think the internet will look like in another 20 years. Again, I have my own ideas, and no, they are nothing like Minority Report.

EEss2s2Sep 06 2009, 10:24 PM
That is very true, your locale and your method of access to the internet greatly determines what you can see and say. In this, I agree wholeheartedly that certain freedoms are restricted. On the whole, many of these restricted "freedoms" are intolerable behavior and criminal activity such as child pornography, terrorist propaganda, and other such affronts. The flipside to this though is in countries such as China and to a certain extent Australia, where the government and ISPs have agreed to filter content that they deem unsuitable. This is true censorship and unfortunately, it's largely out of the hands of the people unless they either topple their governments or find ways to circumvent the use of government/ISP controlled data pipelines.Still, I think that in most cases, the opportunities for free expression in a virtual space far outweigh that afforded to public demonstrations, postings, or other forms of real life expression. One of the reasons is because it's very easy on the internet to create a space you can claim as your own, where you can subsequently do most anything you want. Even if you decide to do things that are frowned upon or not quite legal, the anonymity of the internet makes possible to find a dark corner shared by others with similar unsavory interests and once again, you are able to do as you wish. The reason I think this is true is because in public forums in the real world, you expose yourself to anyone who passes by, making threats, violence, or police action. I agree with Frosty that yeah, you can do most anything you like in the real world in public, granted if you are willing to accept the consequences, but the internet by contrast has fewer consequences, fewer watchdogs, and generally is more cleanly segregated in terms of group interest. This creates a unique environment that encourages more freedom of expression and carries a greater overall sense of general freedom than a non-virtual environment.While there are internet monitoring groups out there, they see varying degrees of success to to the sheer size of the internet and the lock-and-key nature that goes along with certain activities. In an example that crosses over into the real world, Perverted Justice does what I consider a fine job in exposing and helping to capture would be sexual predators by lurking in public chatrooms. In other types of activity that may be monitored by outside groups such as filesharing, private trackers and IP blocking software make uprooting major file sharers much more difficult, and though the watchdogs may enjoy success in certain cases (TPB being a recent major one) for the most part, filesharing goes on largely unhindered.As I mentioned in my original post, the internet is a groundbreaking tool, and is used by such a large cross-section of interests and reaches so many people throughout the world, there is no way I can fathom the return to a non-information based age of man. It only gets bigger from here.As for 20 years from now, I think we'll find more and more seamless ways to integrate with the internet, eventually being connected all the time everywhere we go and being able to call up any information we want at any time. Gadgets which facilitate this will grow smaller, more complex and capable and more integrated into each other as well as ourselves. At one time, there were no phones. You were out somewhere and wanted to make or recieve a call, you had to go find a landline. Now cellphones are ubiquitous and have begun absorbing cameras, movie recorders, PDAs game systems, and the internet into single, pocket sized packages. In 20 years, we may end up getting sub-dermal implants in our skin and eyes that allow us to organically access information and view it privately.

You bring up some interesting points. I don't think that we can ever truly grasp the complete scope of the internet and the possibilities that it affords us. However, I did write an ethnography last year on internet gaming and how the internet as affected linguistics.The internet has allowed individuals from all over the world to connect in new ways and create new communities. This in turn has created new sub-cultures such as 4chan ( as much as I despise it) and other social communities. These communities can include social networking sites like myspace or facebook, but they can also include guilds and linkshells from mmos. The creation of these communities eventually impact everyone's everyday life. This occurs in the form of new words, abbreviations, and shifts in cultural norms. When I was little it was viewed as being strange to be a gamer. Now due in part to mmos it is now more acceptable to play games.The issue with the anonymity argument is that people are never truly anonymous online. A person's location and identity can be gained simply by analyzing their IP address. Despite this point people do post and behave online as if they were anonymous, which ultimately will bite them in the butt.I think that ultimately the internet is neither good nor bad. How people choose to utilize the power governs if it is positive or negative. Pedophiles always existed within our modern culture. The internet DID offer them a new way to find their victims. However, these pedophiles would exist even without the internet. At least with the internet the FBI/Police/Volunteers are able to locate the pedophiles more easily and arrest them.

The internet has provided an extremely valuable way of getting messages out to the general public. In particular this has helped marginalized peoples who have historically been silenced by their oppressors being able to get their message out and in synch with this we have seen NGO's (Non government organisations) springing up and using this medium to champion their causes. As another thread I am (somewhat poorly) demonstrating notions such as progress and development have been shaped largely by ethnocentric perspectives which States are able to feed off in order to justify and continue their exploitation. However with the internet we have seen this being hit more directly with organisations such as survival international, which track crimes committed against tribal peoples. We have also seen this increased connectivity result in fourth world peoples from around the world being able to unite and stand together. While strictly not the internet the same principle applies with their drive which managed to get the declaration of indigenous rights pushed through (albeit in a watered down version) at the United Nations.Even when we turn to countries which are held up as examples where their leaders rule with an Iron fist, namely China and Iran we have seen in recent events that use of the internet is changing the way things are going though. When it comes to Iran, protests against the election results made extensive use of twitter and other social networking sites on the internet to try and get their voices out to the wider international community. Indeed it was through such means that the death of Nada became so symbolic with the oppression of the protesters. We have also seen protesters increasing in numbers in China despite increased security measures. This article from the economist, (very) briefly touches on the use of internet to raise public awareness and support over court cases. Here is a more specific example, where such tactics were able to tone down the punishment of Ms Deng who killed an official attempting to molest her.So from both these points, I would argue that the internet is opening up doors for the marginalized and oppression to better publicize their situations. This is something I strongly endorse. (haha bringing attention to it, not the exploitation itself).
