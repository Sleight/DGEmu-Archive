This is a typical example of consequentialism and utilitarianism. The arguments against this, is primarily the damage it has to minorities and the rights of the individual. Certainly, it would benefit the society if we killed everyone with infectuous disease - but the moral implications of doing so are so vast that it's a ludicrous thing to even suggest. This applies to many other things as well. One could end the conflict between the Palestinians and Israelis by nuking one of the countries - that way one might spare a lot of bloodshed in the future - but it wouldn't be something you could justify doing, because a minority, or a "group" always ends up suffering for it, and you wouldn't want to be part of such a group yourself (or any family member, etc).

WWolf O'DonnellMar 23 2010, 08:42 PM
The end certainly doesn't justify the means and we have a few real life case examples to prove the point.For example, the Afghanistan War... specifically, the Soviet-Afghan War. The US waged war by proxy in this case, covertly helping the Taliban and possibly even training Osama bin Laden. The Soviet Union falls by the wayside and the US makes the mistake of basing troops in Saudi Arabia in order to attack Saddam's Iraq. Next thing you know, Osama bin Laden has turned against the US, is bombing US interests and then the Taliban shelter Osama bin Laden. Now, I may be oversimplifying, but we can all safely say that the US's interference in Afghanistan has caused a few problems.And take Saddam's overthrow. Did the end justify the means? Obviously not. The means was cack-handed and has left a devestating political situation in an already fragile area.The thinking behind "the ends justifies the means" fails to recognise that the goal is not the end. You will have to live with the consequences of your means of obtaining your goal. The US is certainly living with that goal.Heck, the UK and US are living with the consequences of their interference in Iran during the first half of the 20th Century. Helping the overthrow of a civilian democracy in order to gain access to Iranian oil proved to be a disastrous idea. Sure, the UK and US got their oil, but at the cost of Iran eventually descending into an Islamic Theocratic dictatorship.And everything I've cited so far are just the political examples. There's possibly other non-political examples that haven't yet sprung to mind.

I believe that the U.S.A. is a bad example to quote for the efficacy of Realism/Utilitarianism as it is very inconsistent in its application. One moment, it does what it needs to do, the next it pussyfoots around. It's this fickleness in policy that causes the U.S.A. to shoot itself in the foot...as well as the outright lying for the use of certain agendas like for the invasion of Iraq. If they actually were committed to a "Machiavellian" approach, the agencies involved would have circumvented international laws and captured or killed Osama Bin Laden during one of the times they had him in their sights. One cannot be holier-than-thou one moment then steel themselves for real world action in the other and then become righteous again and expect results and responses in line with such a policy as soon as it's applied; both the country's track record and the usual delay in the encountering of effects to its actions will thwart any policy until it's applied religiously and consistently without breaks for an extended period.Interestingly enough, Machiavellian principles were not originated in Il Principe, but finds much precedent in the ancient work Arthashastra which is actually even more comprehensive.
