Ccag3Mar 01 2009, 03:15 AM
Technology seems to occupy the accused position very often. Not too long ago, cell phone calls were deemed a socially isolating commodity. The argument was that the fact that people know they can reach you 24/7 via cell phone not only destroyed a large portion of your privacy, but made people in general less socially healthy. The fact that you could simply call somebody's cell and expect them to answer it made the messages that calls incorporate more evanescent and less urgent. Then again, phones were even accused of making social communication less personal. Letters, then, and now, typically involve more emotional investment, and as the argument presents, were more socially healthy even if less frequent.The fact that all of this still lives on in modern society speaks volumes about our society. I think that what we hold as sacred and everlasting tenants of social communication are not as urgent as we think they be, nor are they required to be. The rampant abbrev. and butchering of standard grammar for brevity isn't such a huge problem, nor is cell phone txting destroying the literacy of our society. The modern English language, and most languages for that matter, is very malleable, and for all purposes, is a practical language. I hold that cell phone txting is just a form of adaptation to new technology; after all, the practice is not that much different from passing notes. If semi-illiterate people lost the ability to text, they certainly wouldn't be writing grammatically correct notes in the first place, nor is it reasonable to assume that people will follow standard English to the letter. If the participants of the communication are communicating well, then how they communicate should generally take back seat to the message.I second the recommendation: Fahrenheit 451 does very much directly address this issue (after all, a central symbolic image of the work is a technologically advanced firemen squad burning books).
