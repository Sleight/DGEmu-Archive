This was something I wrote as part of my portfolio for British Literature I.

It's an epic poem, written using no real meter or rhyme and in a bastardized version of Shakesperian/old English that I affected for the sake of style and nothing else.

Introduction


In years long past, there was a land
governed by a lord, ungoverned himself.
He ruled in terror and proved unworthy of power,
waged war with nobler kings and wrought ruin
upon his kingdom and his high hall.

His son, called Roman, spoke to his warriors,
his wise men, his thanes, crushed under the tyrant.
He promised them fame, honor, and praise
mounds of gold and titles of glory
if but they hurried the king to his end.
A knife, coated with chemist’s concoctions,
was secreted to the foul king’s steward
who sheathed the blade in his master’s side.

The king suffered, venoms coursing in his veins,
bitter agonies before mercy took his breath.
The Roman was seated in his father’s throne
ere it had lost its warmth. His followers,
shamed by their guilt in unholy affairs,
watched as one tyrant gave way to another.
His oaths broken, his enemies cowed,
the patricide proved no nobler than his sire.

From under him arose knights, of equal worth,
themselves the workers of Hades’ great works.
To befit their king, they took the names
of the heathen gods of old. In a land where the law
came forth from the lips of madmen, they sowed
terror and reaped rewards from any unfortunate
who fell across their path. Their chief was Janus,
a knight of noble birth, whose bright ways were
eclipsed by dark deeds done in darker domains.
He spoke of Law, of Truth, but carried none.
He spoke as the Arbiter of Justice, but by his
actions, made a mockery of it.
With him always he carried cards, of Eastern make,
as used by soothsayers in the Arabian wastes.
To any misdeed or quarrel he may find,
he would take Solomon’s place, and act as judge.
Relied he not, though, on word of man,
but on Chance’s fancy. He would draw a card
from that awful deck, and make his judgements thus.
As like to set a killer free as bring God’s justice
to the sinner, and soon as slay the blameless as
he would give them leave.

Jupiter was no better,
and was Satan’s favorite. His lips leaked blasphemies,
his mind cluttered with heresy. He took his name
not out of fashion, but out of madness. Father of gods,
he was called, so father of gods he must be.
From Olympus’ mount in mortal flesh,
he demanded sacrifice to fill his greed, his lust.
Pagan ceremony, pagan blood, pagan hunger,
desecrating God’s holy house, blaspheming upon
the pages of His Word, killing the priests and
slaying the monks, the heretic cursed heaven
and proclaimed paradise on earth. He demanded
tributes, new every day, on whatever his hell-guided
and demon twisted soul laid its eyes upon.
Winter stores spent up in personal feasts,
livestock slaughtered in damnėd sacrifice,
girl’s virtues wasted in unholy desires,
and King Roman showed no pity upon his people.

Last was Pluto, and fewer words said is best.
He was called Charon, Dragon, and Beast.
His skin was accursed, like that of the lizard,
no weapon he used, or needed, than that he
had been born with. More Animal than Man,
he spoke not a word, and followed the King’s
commands like a rabid dog. No law he followed,
save that of the wild beasts, of a hunger never filled.
On a pale horse he rode, hid behind a helm,
through marsh and through forest, leaving behind
the bones of the dead. None dared confront him,
for those that did were not seen in this world again.

The people of this unhappy land cried to the King,
and the King did not hear. The earth swelled with blood
and cried out to Heaven. And Holy God, in His mercy,
did not forget them. As in the days of Israel and his
brother Judah, caught fast in Babylon’s hands,
as the Hebrews were subject to the lash of Pharoah’s whips,
there came a hero, blessed by Heaven, sent by the saints.
As in the days before David, Jesse’s son,
the Almighty brought forth a great Judge,
to answer the question posed by Janus.
A man of no small reason, to calm the fevered
head of the afflicted Jove. A man of such strength
to crack Pluto’s enigma. He carried himself in dark,
but served light. His name was known to God alone,
and his home was of the like.


Janus

The unknown knight enters the land of the Roman and sees the destruction the land has suffered. Vowing to put an end to it, he begins his search for the three knights. During his journey, he comes across a patrol of soldiers who attempt to jail him. The knight draws them out to a cliff, and through craft causes the mounted soldiers to fall to the gulch below.


The sun was soon put to rest as
the knight drew near to where Janus dwelt.
In the yard the Judge held court
passing judgment by virtue of his totem.

Two men stood before his gaze,
their dress base and their carriage low.
The one claimed his purse had been cut
by the second, the coinage taken,
three weeks wages in worth. The second
was found to hold such riches and more,
all earned rightly and none taken in
sinful greed. Janus spoke not a word,
left unstirred in his seat. The knight spoke
to him.

“I am a stranger to this land. Its ways are
unknown to me, your words are strange
and your colors alien to my eyes. I have
walked many miles, seen many stars,
heard tongues too numerous to count,
but I have yet to see a man of law
stay silent in the face of lawlessness.
Tell me, friend, do not even the heathens
know that theft is the act of the guilty?
Do not lies deserve punishment, lest they
the truth should warp? If this man has
his life’s wages stolen, should he be
left to starve? Or, if he should lie to gain
his brother’s gains and shame his noble
spirit, should not his lie reap its rewards?
If justice is not served, it goes blind.
You name yourself judge, but judge not.
How can you claim authority over man
if you will not attend to him?”

Janus touched his cards, and spoke.
“You speak of justice. Tell me, what is it?
Can you show it to me? How does it weigh?
You cannot. Look to the heavens. The stars
move in order, the sun burns her path in the sky
and the moon dies and is born anew in his
time. The fields bloom and wane, the seasons
count off the year. They have order,
they have laws, they are at peace.
What laws does man serve? Can you trace
his path? You speak of justice blinded,
but what proof do you have that it has sight?
Suppose I pass judgment. Suppose I say to
one of these men, ‘You have been wronged.
Let what has been done to you be done to him.’
What then? The thief will steal anew. He stole
in hunger, and the hunger is left unfed. He stole
in greed, and the greed is not gone. If I put him
in chains, he will only grow to hate his bonds,
not the sins that put him in them. If I kill him,
he dies a thief. And suppose I judge wrong?
What then, when the innocent suffer for
the wounds they are afflicted with?
Justice is a fancy, spoken to comfort children.
But when sweet lying lips fall silent, what
would you have me do? I have made my choice.
The lives of men are too much to hold in my hands.
From this deck I draw a card, so that God may
choose, if He cares. Look, as I do it now.
I drew the Fool. Chance has spoken, wayfarer,
and whispered your name in my ear. Where you
are from, why are you are here, these are vapors.
Do not speak of innocence. That you were not
here to perform the act matters little. You are a man,
you have done something to deserve this death.”

He took his weapon in hand, and would have made
an end to the unnamed knight. But where once was
flesh, now was air. The knight drew steel, and with
Janus clashed.

Janus and the unnamed knight have begun their duel. During a lull in the fight, the knight attempts to reason with Janus.
“You spoke of stars and men. The paths the stars
follow is known to all who watch in patience.
Their arcs have been ruled by divine hands
in perfect arcs such as no man can mark.
But you speak as a man, with a man’s eyes,
making judgment on the sky from a distance.
Amongst the stars, were you to put your head
into the ethers you would not find order.
No star knows the beauty of the dance it makes
amidst its uncounted kindred, but plays its role.
The One who put the celestial mechanism in place
alone is afforded the knowledge and vision.
The part does not contain the whole.
Justice often seems lacking, but ‘seems’ is what
we must mark. Justice is not blind, Janus: we are.”

Janus spoke in return. “If I do wrong, what now?
My blood remains in my veins, not spilled,
my neck remains firmly rooted to my body,
and my victims lie on blood soaked ground.
My life makes a lie of your pretty words.”

He made to end the knight’s life, but in anger
struck wild at air. A blade flashed as Janus fell.
From cold hands fell the tarot and the cards
soaked the leaking life of the fallen judge.
The unnamed knight spoke, before the light
would pass from the dulling eyes.
“Let my words not be dead air in dead ears.
Know that your past faith in the laws of man
was not in vain. I weep that your life must end
under the throes of this dark madness.
God as my witness, no other man will share
this end with you.” Janus said not a word,
his sorry soul’s earthly stay had been ended.

The tale of the knight’s deed rode fast
on the tongues of men, and Janus’ fall
was soon in the Roman King’s court.
Dark was his humor and darker his mind.
Thus said the king, “This is no knight.
No color he wears, no sign does he carry,
but a living void, shaped by the ignorance
of my men. I will not sleep until this man
is broken, beaten, his life stolen and his pride
stomped into the dust. Every day he lives
will be an eternity of hell, and no man
who swears oath to me shall rest ‘till
his blood stains the ground!”

Jupiter spoke to him. “Jove hears, O Lord!
This mortal shall fall at his immortal hands!
One stroke of thunder will put thee aright.”

The king spoke in anger. “Jupiter, attend me!
Temper your madness before I strike you,
and lose another of my soldiers. Put away your
accursed pipe before the vapors choke
what little reason you have left in you.
But attend to me, for I know my action.
I shall send Pluto to make an end to this.”


Pluto


The knight went further down the road
ever winding its end to the Roman’s gates,
hidden at the feet of mighty white mountains
rising like jagged ribs bursting through earth.
He traversed through the marshlands,
deigning not to rest his wearied body
while his soul burned him from within.

From the east a wave of fog came forth
blanketing the land in smoke thick as blood.
To every hole and nest and every safe place
fled all of God’s creation that had wisdom,
knowing that the dark hid those things,
monstrous and vile and unspeakable,
who hate the light and all who live by it.
From out of the dark corners of the earth
they spread, from their hidings in the places
where neither sun nor moon nor star could gaze,
searching for flesh to rend and blood to spill.
Chief among them rode Pluto, the hunter.
From within his cavernous helm, he sniffed,
guiding his plague-ridden steed to his quarry.
The unknowing knight strove ahead,
a burning branch in hand, warding off
the many legged things that stalked him
in hunger but fled in fear of the flames.

From the darkness Pluto struck mightily
with speed the knight could not match.
The fearsome foe retreated to the dark
as the knight fought to hold aloft his light,
searching for the source of unseen aggression.
From his flank an arm flashed against him
and threw him down to his feet. The burning
rod fell from him, and things bore upon him
with teeth and pincers and claws and malic,
but they were dismayed by the steel he drew
and he taught them the terrors of death.
The knight’s victory was short lived
as Pluto struck once more from behind.
Fear flooded his legs but his will was
like iron and his spirit not so soon broken.
He stood still, listening to the environs,
and perceived a presence before him.
The Beast carried death with him,
the harsh stench of the slain lay heavy
upon the crude armor that covered a man
who was long lost to reason.
The knight stood at breast height to him,
though Pluto’s legs were bowed, his head down
his shoulders crouched and his stance low.
The Beast spoke none, his mouth as monstrous
as the man. The knight did not waste his breath,
but struck heavily. The blade bit deep and true
upon his breast yet the Beast stood strong.
Hands wrought from iron clamped tight upon
the knight, seeking to crush life from him
Man and Beast struggled, Pluto’s mighty grip
holding fast and the knight striking out
against what targets he had with his feet.
In silence they fought, the knight unable to utter
and the Beast breathing hard, harder, hardest
as his life spilled to the ground. From beneath
his helm the knight could see naught but dumb rage,
and then naught and nothing more. The Beast
succumbed to its wound, and fell to earth and
carried the knight with it, its death grip as potent
as in life. The knight broke free of the lifeless
fingers and looked down upon the fallen god
and pried his blade from its breast. He turned
once more to the road, leaving Pluto to the
mercies of what things that live in the dark.


Jupiter

After his encounter with Pluto, the unnamed knight rested in a village outside of the swamp. During his stay, a scop challenged him first to a contest of riddles. After both told a riddle the other couldn’t answer, a tie was declared. To break it, the two agreed that the one who told the greater tale would win. The scop goes first, recounting the story of Sigurd, who slayed the dragon. The knight counters with the winning tale of the construction of the Tower of Babel, and the consequences that followed it.
His wounds healed and his spirit refreshed, he journeys to the gates of the Roman, where Jupiter has come out to meet him.
Jove spoke in fevered tones, lost in madness.
“Behold the sight that you see, heathen!
I am the Divine, the Eternal, the Omnipotent.
Abuse your mind not at all, oh man, for my
Glories are merely hidden away, so that
your petty mind might not unwind.
Get gone, and you may yet speak to your
children and your countrymen of how
you came to look upon Perfection and lived.
This boon will mighty Jupiter grant to you.”

The unknown knight spoke in turn.
“You give me warning, and I will do so in turn.
Turn away now. Put your back to this place,
and never turn back. My path is through you.
If you do not step aside of your own will,
then I will be forced to move you myself.”

Jupiter spoke. “You dare threaten a god?
Mighty Jove has all the earth upon his palm.
The elements are my subjects, the humours
of man my play things. You are as a gnat
gnawing upon the hide of a mighty stallion.
And I am a mighty stallion indeed!”

The knight spoke. “I will not speak in riddles.
My purpose is terrible, my determination awful,
my will ready, my mind clear, my sword steady.
I have slain Janus, I have conquered Pluto.
Tell me, Jupiter, what hope do you have?
I do not see a dread warrior before me,
nor a crafty swordsmen. I see a man lost
in the haze of vapors. I see a man trapped
in a fantasy. No, I speak falsely, I do not
see a man, I see a boy playing games and
thinking them real. Leave now, or by God
and all the saints, I will put an end to you,
here and now, as you stand!”

What courage Jupiter had in him fled
and with it he took flight.


The Roman
The portal of that mighty house opened wide
and out from it spewed the guard of the King
the bull rampant upon their shields and hearts
and faceless under their masks of black
and they encased the knight within a cage of
spears and swords, hammer and mace.
The knight was unmoved by their strength
and moved not at all from his place, but looked
forward into the house of their lord.
From the maw of the hall came the Roman King
bloody blade and naked fury ready in hand.

He spoke to the knight. “What do I see before me?
Not a great army of battle-born soldiers and knights,
nor a band of warriors under oath to a great hero.
I see a man, merely a man, dressed in tattered rags!
Is this the source of my restless nights and fears?
With a word, I could loose a thousand strings
and throw a hundred spears and finish my foe
in the beat of a heart, the power of a master of men.
But be still, my servants. I wish to know what
courses of fate brought about this meeting.”

The knight spoke. “Your tyranny’s end, oh Roman,
has brought us to this place and present enmity.
You rule as an invalid, no heed for your people,
madmen under your fealty murder by your leave,
and befitting your blood-stained reign
you began it in blood, poisoning your father
and took his throne before warmth left his body.”

The Roman King spoke in reply. “What of it, then?
I shed the blood of others to further my ends,
even to the point of patricide by poison,
but what we yearn for must be paid for dearly.
If I had to slay my armies and peasants at the altar,
I would have done so with cheer. Power is earned
by those who take it, not given to the meek who
bow their heads and hold out hands to receive it.
I will not apologize for power gained and used,
and speak true my friend. You know power.
How many souls have you extinguished by hand,
using the power of your arm? If I am guilty
for commanding my men to kill, you are guilty for
commanding your body too.”
The knight spoke again. “Power was given,
but power that serves itself reaps death.
I am not a wolf wild amongst the herd
nor have I given the innocent to Death’s arms.
I have slain every man you send against me
who does not seek life away from my blade.
The end of my quest ends with you, Roman,
and until that time I will not die by any hand.
But I ask this to you, King, what of your men?
How many must you sacrifice, before they
forget your of you power, and they turn on
the king who sends them to certain death?
How long before the abuses of a king
who loves them not turns from the crack of
the whip to the sucking of the leech
and they pluck you from your skin and
toss you the ground like a worm?”

The company of masked men turned
to the man who spoke to them as king,
but their ears had grown deaf to him.
The Roman was put to his end,
and his hall left crumbling to the winds
as the land grew green and forgot
what ravages were brought to it
by the tramp of iron-clad feet.
The knight disappeared in total,
no sign of him left upon the land
but the stories that were sung
of the Roman and his pantheon.
