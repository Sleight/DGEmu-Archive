To an extent.The wording of the American dream is slightly vague, but my interpretation could be summed up like this; to make something more of yourself than you could be in other environments, and to be free.When the phrase was coined, America had much more social mobility. The well-founded companies and businesses we have today, who more or less decide where half the money in the world ends up, were either young or nonexistant. Where now, you'll find a Walmart in every city, and Starbucks on every street corner, the lack of monopolies in those days allowed for the sort of small businesses that rarely last a year, now. And then, there was the simple matter that with so much free space, it was less expensive to own land than nearly anywhere else in the world.On top of this, there were issues such as religious freedoms and politics. I'm no history buff, so I couldn't say when these things became more liberal in Europe, but I'm sure the concept of practicing whatever religion you want and electing your leaders led to a perceived improved quality of life. These days, though? America's old and wealthy enough that now it has its own nobility long since entrenched. Sure, there are still people who come from meager beginnings and end up filthy stinking rich, through music or those hideous, baffling Croc shoes, but I can't imagine that's very much more frequent than any other first world country, and it's still going to depend more on your connections or blind luck than hard work and perseverence. So yes, and no. The American dream, or what it seems to mean, can happen for plenty of people. But it's not universal, and it isn't nearly as limited to America as it used to be.
