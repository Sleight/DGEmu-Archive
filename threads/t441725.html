EEss2s2Sep 02 2009, 04:11 AM
Banning breeds of animals is a reactionary measure, taken by people who have been exposed to some form of negative incident with the breeds in question. Few cities ever hold a public vote for municipal ordinances, most of those (such as a breed ban within city limits) are put to a vote on the city council, which is not a motion that is open to public discourse.The problem I see with a breed ban is that law-abiding (and largely considerate and responsible) owners of the breeds in question will lose what many consider to be a treasured family member. On the flip side of the coin, many of the owners of banned animals may be irresponsible or neglectful, leading to the animal being the aggressive archetype that is the root cause of said ban. And will likely ignore the ordinance and continue to retain ownership of the banned animals and might start up underground puppy mills to satisfy the desire for the outlawed breeds. This seems like another one of those laws that creates more crime than it stops, simply for the fact that it's a poorly thought out law created by people with selfish reasons for enacting said law.As said above, most if not all breeds of dogs can be raised to be gentle, despite perceived genetic or instinctual predisposition. For some reason I cannot find any references with any veracity that says pit bulls are genetically predisposed to violent behavior. In fact, in many of the articles I've referenced (see below for sources), historically pit bulls which were trained and bred to fight were also trained specifically not to bite their handlers, regardless of how ensconced the dogs were in the throes of fighting one another. Such breeds (including rottweilers and doberman pinschers) have genetic tools which make them good fighters, but whose attitudes are by and large the product of their environment. Pit bulls have gone from being misunderstood to garnering a poor reputation among many people through the propagation of misinformation and publicized incidences of poorly behaved animals at the hands of irresponsible owners. I'm singling out pit bulls as a point of reference, but any "questionable" breed is equally subject to the validity of these points.Many people like to point out that the AKC does not recognize the breed pit bull and love to cite that that is because the dog is so aggressive and so the AKC doesn't want to lend favor to such an uncontrollable breed. Wrong. The pit bull's true breed name is the American Staffordshire Terrier, and is not only recognized by both the AKC and the UKC, but is prized for its ability to accept obedience training and its affectionate and loyal nature.I agree with the above stated sentiment that these city ordinances are "shotgun" laws intended to make a perceived problem go away with the least amount of effort needed. When in fact targeted training for potential pet owners and low-cost owner resources such as obedience training and breed-specific care programs would do leagues more to improve the welfare of both the city and the dogs. These measures could conceivable be enacted for the same cost or even less than the cost of enacting and enforcing breed bans.As an addendum, the AKC also condemns the breed bans on pit bulls and other such breeds because those bans inevitably lead to mass euthanasia of seized outlawed breeds within the city limits due to the near impossibility to place such animals outside the territorial limits of the ban. For owners, for dogs, and for the city at large, an outright ban is harmful for all parties involved.
