In particular, I would consider him an ideal leader until 1981, and an adequate one until 1990.Let us start from the beginning, with the political situation in Iraq before the rise of Saddam.In 1958, the last pro-western Iraqi king, King Faisal II, was overthrown and executed. The new government was lead by a group of military officers inspired by Nasser Arafat's idea of Arab unity. A direct result of this was a breakup of relations with Jordan, itself ruled by a pro-western monarch. And because of this, none of Iraq's neighbors were on their side.Imperial Iran was quick to take advantage of this. The river making up the dividing line in the south of the two countries was called the 'Shatt al-Arab' by Iraq. This simply means 'Arab River' (lit: River of the Arabs), which itself did not ease the situation (the Iranians just called it Arvand Rūd, or Arvand river). Iraq was in a highly weak position; it lacked the military strength of Iran, and was internally unstable (three coups and five different governments from '58-68). A Baathist government finally took over in 1968, bringing stability.But that was not all. In 1970 Baghdad accused Tehran of backing a coup against it. Tehran pushed deeper, backing Kurdish rebels in the north. In addition, with the new absence of British power in the Persian gulf, Iran wanted to let Iraq know that it was indeed Persian.Baghdad then tried to turn toward Moscow, breaking off relations with Tehran and London. It the cold-war here as well; radical, pan-Arabist, pro-Soviet Iraq, versus monarchical, pro-Israeli, pro-American Iran.Some of the ice broke in 1973, when Iraq thought that Israel was a greater threat than Iran. But this did not stop a Kurdish uprising in 1974. Iran publicly backed this uprising with troops, and American and Israeli made weaponry.And thus, the rise of Saddam Hussein came.It is far more interesting to look at previous history books than present ones, because they show the true face. While Iraq suspected Iran of all-out war, "the Shah had no such intention, particularly as he observed the rise of Saddam Hussein. This young man...became vice-president and gradually [nudged the president] out of the real seat of power during the early seventies."1While Saddam Hussein was committed to the idea of pan-Arabism, he disliked having to ask the Soviets for weaponry, particularly because they did not generally provide them. For example, in 1974, despite an all-out uprising in Kurdistan, the Iraqi air force allegedly only had three bombs. The Shah, also worried about his own Kurdish minority, was not all willing to continue supporting the idea of Kurdistan. In the end, an exhausted and weak Iraq gave up some of its rights and naval space in the Shatt al-arab and the Persian gulf as a whole.In the end, however, Iraq gained from this. The Kurds, realizing they were abandoned, were quickly oppressed. In addition, the new government, facing Shi'a discontent due to economic and social inequality, was now able to throw money at it (i.e. industrialize these areas, among other things).By now, of course, Saddam was pretty much in control. Economic increases in Iraq war enormous. In 1972, Iraqi oil income was $75 million; in 1975, $8000 million. The state continued to modernize, with Baathist members responsible for the safety of their street or neighborhood. The Shi'a problem is what a lot of people criticize Saddam for, so I'll tackle that. The Shi'a have long not had high standing in Baghdad; the Ottomans outright excluded them from public office. Shi'a themselves were not allowed to have any judicial power save for internal matters. That said, Najaf and Karbala, which contain the shrines of both Imam Ali and his son Imam Hussein (the highest figures in the Shi'a religion aside from Muhammed), were allowed to be semi-independent cities.The Baathist party quickly lost popularity among the Shi'a, when , dominated by Sunnis, it tried to interfere with Shi'a rituals and label it cultural before religious. In 1974, Shi'a protests reached a din, led by the al-Daawa al-Islamiya (the Islamic call). The government did what any other developing country would have done; started rolling heads (25 Shi'a leaders were tried, five executed). In 1977, this repression was thrown upward by protests when Imam Hussein's shrine in Karbala was sealed off (the Iraqi government suspected a Syrian (the two governments were then hostile) agent of attempting to smuggle explosives into it). An Iraqi police station was token over. Again, developing countries didn't commonly have riot gear back then, which didn't really calm protests.Saddam, loyal to the cause of the secular Baathist revolution (unlike the recent wave of Islamic revival, which claimed more than a few Iraqi politicians), choose to give the Shi'a a choice, so to speak. The first was to cooperate with the government, and keep their grievances out of the streets. The general levels of the Baathist party were expanded to Shi'a, economic prosperity reigned, and increased political representation was hinted at. The second was to take the path of al-Daawa, which led to oppression, torture (not as in 'Guantanamo-bay' torture, but as in 'slowly-break-the-vertebrae-of-your-victim'2 torture), the whole nine yards. I want to respond to your second essay, about the Iraq War as compared to other occupations and wars. On the whole, I think you'll find my ideas rather cop-outish, but then again, there are no right answers, right?I believe one of the reasons the several civil wars and occupations you listed have been such long and bloody struggles is precisely because they did not have the Western idea of moral warfare and the Geneva Convention. It is true that enormous force can quickly flush out enemies; we've observed as much from the German blitzkrieg and our own easy victory against Saddam Hussein's forces. However, when facing entire populations, one cannot just completely destroy all enemies; by killing one enemy, that act spawns more men to join in fighting against you. It has been true with all wars and occupations; attrition adds up. What is the solution? Well, I hardly want to flatter myself into believing that I have a solution to any aspect of the Iraq War, but moral warfare and preserving human rights is certainly a start. The United States does not hate Iraqis: we are not the French, who treated the Algerians as second class citizens. We are not the ethnic cleansers, blood rivals and other such groups in the civil wars you mentioned. If we can convince the Iraqis that we're there to help by some minimal respect of human rights and the other values we've sort of trumpeted loudly and tried to impose on Iraq (as we have, by and large, been doing), we can make some progress. But if we engage in all out attacks - our numerous offensives against Sadr's forces being the prime example - even in victory, we only give greater reason and publicity to an enemy that can then quickly recruit new members and strike again. By engaging in more defensive tactics aimed, for example, at al-Qaeda, which was a threat to both Americans and Iraqis, we can demonize such forces in the eyes of the common people. Without the common people's support, their home-court advantage, so to speak, becomes severely reduced and so they become all the weaker. As for a complete withdrawal, I do agree that Vietnam, our prime example, has hardly come back to bite us: the domino theory turned out to be drastically wrong, and economic liberalization has done wonders for the rest. I would actually believe that no regional powers can actually come in and fill any power vacuum: again drawing from the al-Qaeda example, Iraqis were quick to resent these foreigners who were suddenly imposing their power, even if it was against the supposed common American enemy. But in a time of dramatically low international support and moral resources, especially without our Cold War superpower buffer, we are just inviting greater influence on the part of China and the EU. In terms of grand strategy, I believe that would be a bad play and only speed up the seemingly inevitable catching-up of these two blocs.

However, one way it can alleviate the destructive chain is to allow the discontented to speak and participate. When they feel they can express their discontent within the current system, they will not be so tempted to do so violently. This need for the humanization of the occupying forces and local involvement has been a major prong of Petraeus's new strategy, and I would say it has been working quite well relative to our previous super-aggressive strategies.
