Some personal favourites:
Bradbury, Ray - Fahrenheit 451: Simple writing and uninteresting characters, but very compelling.

Camus, Albert - The Stranger: Daaaaaaarker than you can imagine. You'll want to commit suicide after this because you'll realise you don't have any free will and even if you did, every single shitty thing in life is destined to happen anyway and you can't do a thing about it. But hey, if a book can do that, it's pretty powerful right?
Capote, Truman - In Cold Blood: This one won't make you very happy either. But it's a chilling, gripping crime non-fiction novel.
Coelho, Paulo - The Alchemist: More symbolism than you can shake a stick at! Simple but brilliant writing.
Coetzee, J.M. - Disgrace: Dude won the Nobel Prize for this. It's pretty good.
Defoe, Daniel - Robinson Crusoe: It's goddamn Robinson Crusoe, this **** is awesome and if you haven't read it you're dumb.
Ellis, Bret Easton - American Psycho: Book is even better than the movie. Not as gruesome and evil as all the controversy makes you believe though.
Hawking, Stephen - A Brief History of Time: This will make you smarter. But you have to use your brain. A lot.
Hosseini, Khaled - A Thousand Splendid Suns: Better than the Kite Runner which is kind of overrated imo.
Marquez, Gabriel Garcia - Love in the Time of Cholera: Marquez is my favourite writer ever. Just read every single book of his.
McCarthy, Cormac - Blood Meridian: So very bleak. Very violent. Extremely sombre. Staggeringly brilliant.
McCarthy, Cormac - The Road: Because McCarthy is just awesome. This one might even be bleaker. I'm pretty sure this book can break a person.
Orwell, George - 1984: Blah blah timeless classic that everyone should read blah
Rothfuss, Patrick - The Name of the Wind: Mary Sue wish fulfillment fantasy, but still a pretty fun and easy read
Saramago, Jose Blindness: One of my favourite books ever! Saramago has an extremely interesting style that's hard to get used to, but once you do the pages start flying. Like most books I posted this one is dark and depressing, humans suck a lot, etc. It does have a (kind of) happy ending though!
Vonnegut, Kurt - Slaughterhouse-Five: Kind of hard to describe. It's a very strange book. But I've never met someone who didn't like it.

MMo­lh.May 18 2012, 05:41 AM
Hmm, looks like I should put a more complete list.

Books I have read and loved (Note: Long. Really Long.)
Fiction
Kurt Vonnegut - Cat's Cradle; Mother Night; The Sirens of Titan: I am a massive Vonnegut fan. I like basically everything he writes, including his books, speeches, introductions to other people's books, appearances on late-night TV...I like these three the most.
Gaiman/Pratchett: Similarly, I can read just about anything Gaiman. I haven't gone into Pratchett much, somehow, but I've loved everything I've read that's by him, so far, too. Good Omens is certainly an excellent and entertaining book.
Laura Gallego Garcia's The Legend of the Wandering King: I just picked it up a few weeks ago. Theme of a Wanderer having it all, and then losing everything, and then finding himself while atoning for his sins...It's good.
Roger Zelazny - Isle of the Dead, Lord of Light, and The Chronicles of Amber series: Zelazny has the gift of fleshing out fantasy worlds very, very realistically.
Robert Pirisig - Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance: A refreshingly unpretentious take on genius, philosophy, the Art of doing things...and mental illness.
Antoine de Saint Exupery - The Little Prince: What we can learn from the perspective of a child. What we have forgotten since we cast off that perspective.
Hunter S Thompson - Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas: A rollicking good tale.
Franz Kafka - The Metamorphosis: A short and haunting tale on the fragility of community and goodwill.
N. K. Jemisin - The Inheritance Trilogy: On Gods, and how they affect men...and how men reflect them. Astute observations on both people, and the idea of (non-monothestic) godhood.
David Wong - John Dies at the End: A funny and self-aware piece, blending elements of videogame culture, pop culture, movie cliches, and modern life.
Ernest Hemingway - The Old Man and the Sea: A stoic, contemplative old man goes on his travails. We all learn from it.
Ferdowski - The Epic of Kings: A truly epic and exciting tale, still refreshing despite its age.
The Book of the Thousand and One Nights: Already mentioned above.
Alexander Dumas - The Count of Monte Cristo: I have not read the full (and very lengthy) version, just an abridged one when I was very young; but even so it was a compelling tale, exciting from start to finish.
Douglas Adams - The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy series: Smart, funny, entertaining, smooth. Genius.
Alan Moore - V for Vendetta: The portrait of a man who was broken by a society, who has come to break it in turn.
Ursula K Le Guin - The Left Hand of Darkness and The Ones Who Walk Away From Omelas: The former talks about a society without gender; the latter is a short story on the ones who decide to leave a "perfect" society...with a deep and shameful core.
Michael Moorcock: The Elric series, and Behold the Man. The former is about a doomed prince destined to bring about armageddon (and his colourful, gradually-darkening adventures); the latter talks about a man obsessed with the idea of Jesus who travels to the past to find him...and becomes the Man himself.
Voltaire - Candide: A cheerful, hilarious, farcial tale about an overly-optimistic idiot.

Non-Fiction
Doris Lessing - Prisons We Choose to Live Inside: Social commentary, and experiences of a lifetime. Sharp, accurate, yet not critical in tone at all.
David Foster Wallace - This Is Water: A speech on what it truly means to think for yourself and be free. It's here: http://moreintellige...n-his-own-words
Takuan Soho - The Unfettered Mind: Explains Zen (and by extension, Buddhism) more clearly and completely than anything else I've come across. It's here: http://kendo.home.ro...ttered_Mind.pdf
Dale Carnegie - How to Stop Worring and Start Living: Seems bullshitty at first, and that is not helped by the liberal usage of anecdotes, like a used-car salesman chatting you up. The difference is, what he says is actually true. Interesting hmm.
Steven Pressfield - The War of Art: A crusade against procrastination, against running away from your life's work/true destiny. Also, a good read.
Timothy Feriss - The 4-Hour Workweek: How to truly work (and live) for yourself, independent of the rat race. Breezy style, easy-to-read. Hard to follow, mainly because it is so completely empty of bullshit.
One of those excellent, well-written books on Skepticism. Mine was a Martin Gardner book, but I've heard that Carl Sagan's The Demon-Haunted World is good, too. Helps if you are interested in independent thinking, of any kind.
Richard Feynman - The Pleasure of Finding Things Out: The pleasure of science and knowledge, and of hearing about the life and adventures of a truly extraordinary and fun-filled human being.
