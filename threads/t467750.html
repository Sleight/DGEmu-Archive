DdidgeballOct 14 2011, 10:17 PM
I think that the balance of power would generally be better served by the existence of unions. Employees tend to depend on a primary job for their living. Changes to the conditions of this job can greatly affect an employee's standard of living, as well as how much economic activity they generate through their own discretionary spending. The wages and benefits that an employee would negotiate for from the employer are quite directly related to the employee's own life, and thus the costs and benefits are closely linked to the decision-maker. On the other hand, to a (private sector) employer, the employee is two things: a source of benefit (labor) and an accompanying cost (wages). In times of economic stress, it has been possible (as it has been recently) to squeeze more work out of a smaller pool of employees and increase productivity than to hire more workers, even if doing the latter would improve the community as a whole, since these benefits are not part of the microeconomic considerations of the employer. For a for-profit company, thus, the costs are directly related to the decision-maker but the benefits are less related, and so the employer would be expected to think more about the costs of labor. (That's not to say labor is cheap. Between job training, insurance benefits, and other factors, manpower is quite expensive.) As for public unions in particular, the picture is a bit muddier. Governments are typically nonprofit entities, and so do not follow the same profit motives as a private company would. However, many governments are running or expecting budget deficits, which could be alleviated by either cutting services or squeezing its workers. Still, I don't believe that the answer is to go wantonly union-busting. This just produces a lot of ill will and muddies the picture with lots of politics--and I don't think anyone would contend that Scott Walker's and John Kasich's actions in Wisconsin and Ohio aren't politically motivated, considering how much of a grudge the Republican Party has against unions in general (not just public sector unions). On the other hand, Governor Dan Malloy of Connecticut proposed a reasonable set of concessions from state's labor unions, and while some members had misgivings, the unions actually came around to agreeing to them, and even changing their member voting rules so that they could pass them. I've even heard some liberal activists complaining that the concessions are too deep and even some claims that the only reason the unions haven't hated Malloy is because he's a Democrat, so I don't doubt that the concessions are pretty substantial. So, without knowing the details of these negotiations, I'd say that at the least, going out union-busting is not a good idea, and that negotiating and keeping tempers down is a much better way of producing a viable solution. On one hand we have a public sector union going out of its way to approve a package of concessions in Connecticut, and on the other hand we have the union-busting bill in Ohio up for a citizen's repeal vote this November. I'd say the first is more productive.
