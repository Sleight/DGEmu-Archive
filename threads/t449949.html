TTHE GRAND GALACTIC INQUISITORMar 25 2010, 04:39 AM
If I can make sense of this using my own experiences...Cramming for tests just beforehand and then regurgitating the information can lead to good grades, but it can also lead to poor retention of the information. If the content of the course is (considered to be) useless to the student, this isn't a big deal and is probably more likely. If it's really crucial stuff that he or she may use later in life, such as in a vocational setting, this can be disastrous. It's like why more and more graduates with computer science degrees balk during interviews when asked to solve a simple programming question - they memorized the necessary code to pass the tests and then dismissed the information soon after.On another note, you mention that some kids study to the point of suffering and still do poorly compared to those who hardly study at all and pass with flying colors. You refuse to believe that it could be due to each person's ability to grasp the information and/or recall it in a clear manner at test time, but there is some truth to that claim. To a certain extent, some people are "geniuses" and others have to work hard to achieve the same result. If you are one of those who fall into the first category, it may be difficult for you to notice this difference because, to you, getting good grades comes easy. I, for instance, have had good grades all through school and I am close to graduating college now. It has been my experience that I can grasp just about any subject so long as I focus on it and make an effort, but I see others who do just as much and still struggle. In some situations, people just have a knack for a particular ability while others must train and devote large amounts of time and effort to do just as well.All that having been said, if there's one thing I have learned in the sixteen or so years I have spent in the educational system, it's that the goal of the student should not be to pass tests. Students should strive to learn, to understand information and internalize it for later use. Passing tests should just be a byproduct of that goal, a means of gauging one's learning rather than the end toward which one works. The way the system is set up does not really emphasize this important distinction, but if a student can come away from a class feeling that he or she knows more now than before the class started, that should be more important than whether or not he or she did well on the tests.

Your observation as to those who don't learn seem to get better grades is a phenomenon often noticed, but can be explained in many ways. Where I went to school, from grades 6-12, our education was based around standardized testing. To be blatantly honest, passing those tests with a perfect score or close took nothing more than good test taking skills. With the exception of the random essay thrown in, you had questions with answer choices laid out for you. 25% chance right there of blindly guessing the correct answer, but they make it even easier. Two out of the four answer choices are easily eliminated by just reading the question, so your chances skyrocket to 50%. So even walking into these tests blind, you've got the tools to get a 50 or better on a 100 point scale, but because of the reduced skill level of the tests, the vast majority who take it get 80% or better correct. This required no actual learning, just basic test taking skills.
