[font="Book Antiqua"]The idea for this debate, came as I was reading through the end of 'Evolution vs. Creationism'. It reminded me of a more general argument that's been around, just about as long as logical reasoning. The question of 'why' and of 'how'.Let's start at the (hypothetical) start of mankind. We evolved, and discovered the wheel and fire. But we did not understand these things we'd discovered until much later. Ancient philosophers were probably the first to reason logically. Zeno of Elea, in an attempt to prove that all motion is an illusion, proposed a 'paradox'. Achilles and a Tortoise are in a foot race. The tortoise is given a head start of say 100 metres. Let's assume that Achilles is running only twice as fast as the tortoise crawls. In this scenario, you'd expect that Achilles will overtake the tortoise after 200m. But, according to Zeno, to do this, he must first reach the place the tortoise started from. But by then, the tortoise would've moved ahead. So he'd have to catch up again.Everytime he reaches one of these places, the tortoise's lead halves, but it's always ahead. He'll never catch up. The mathematical extrapolation for this idea is an 'infinite series'.This is a paradox because using your common sense, you know that he'll pass it. So how do we describe that motion logically? Archimedes had an answer later, but not until Newton did we have a comprehensive one - the concept of a limit. (of infinite sums)Theorists, scientists, philosophers - these people are always proposing new ideas relevant to the interpretation of commonly known 'facts'. Many people disagree with them, arguing that their proofs are not conclusive or that their ideas are unimportant without physical evidence. What do you think?p.s - you can read more about zeno's paradoxes here:Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy[/font]

I think you've hit the nail on the head when you pointed out Zeno's paradox. He philosophised with an incomplete knowledge of real world mechanics. We now know of quanta, that you can't divide energy into infinitely small pieces; energy comes in discrete "packets". The idea has extended to time itself, saying that even time is quantum, that time cannot be divided indefinitely and that eventually you will reach the smallest packet of time you can get.It's all very well philosophising about this and that, but without an anchor in the real world, philosophy is useless. Mathematicians have come up with the idea that not all infinite sets of numbers are equal in size. Let's say you have a set of every single even number that ever existed. How big is it? Well, it's infinitely big, because whatever is the largest number you can think up of, you can always add two to get a larger even number. Is the set of even numbers as big as the whole set of numbers? They say no, because you can pair all the even number with whole numbers and they match up perfectly. However, then they say, you can't do this with fractions.With fractions, you can't pair up the fractions with whole numbers.Say you have 0.01, 0.02, 0.03 etc. You could potentially pair up the whole numbers, but there will always be an extra fraction in between that screws this up. So there's a 0.015 in between 0.01 and 0.02.Now this is all very mind-boggling and it leads to the famous Hilbert's Paradox of the Grand Hotel, which logically makes no sense as far as I can tell. The Paradox is that you have a hotel with an infinite number of rooms. Each one is full. Some guests come along. The paradox states that all you have to do is move each room's guest to the next one along and you'll free up room number 1. Keep doing it until there are enough rooms for the extra guests. It doesn't matter if the extra guests that come along are of a countably finite or infinite number, this technique is supposed to work.Really? I would have thought that it was not possible to do this because if you were to try to move one guest into the next room, you couldn't, because that room is occupied. And you can't do it for the next room, because that one is also occupied and so forth. Yet I have had some mathematicians argue that I'm treating the rooms as finite. But I argue that they are treating the guests as finite. Ultimately with no basis in reality, who is to say what is right? In fact, what use is this? What use is this idea?We could argue until the cows came home, yet reach no satisfactory conclusion and do nothing.There's one problem though. Arguably, I say there's no use for this particular mathematical idea because I can't conceive of one. Who, however, is to say that one won't appear in the future? It's a big dilemma. How can you show that something is useless and should be disregarded, unless you know everything and see everything? All I can say is that I think it is nonsense, but I am willing to accept that it might not be should I be proven wrong. And that is more than some people are willing to do.
