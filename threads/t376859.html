Member999' post='4110067 wrote on May 3 2008, 04:19 PM:
Dragon Warrior was one of the originals, but also Zelda helped make game developers realize just how popular RPGs were. Additionally there was some knights of the realm game that was pretty fun for its day.
That may be right except that Zelda is NOT an RPG. That's the problem nowadays, people seem to call every game that has even the slightest bit of RPG elements an RPG. I know there are a number of games (like Star Ocean) that blend the genres but back in the day you had clear-cut classifications. They went like this:RPG - You use a menu for everything. To fight. To talk to people. To interact with stuff. Often involved you going to dungeons, fighting battles and getting treasures. You never directly control the character and always use a menu to tell your character to do things. Think Dragon Warrior and Final Fantasy.Adventure - This genre had elements of RPGs where you find items and use them to unlock puzzles and often involved dungeon crawling as well. The difference was while RPGs used menus, almost everything in adventure games were controlled directly by the player. Movement and fighting in particular. Obviously Zelda is a perfect example.Action - This is pure action. No RPG elements. You directly control the character and go through stage after stage until you beat the final boss and beat him. Pretty straightforward. The original Mario games fit this bill.So, for the last time, Zelda is an ADVENTURE game, not an RPG. It never was one and it isn't one now. Stevo

DdidgeballFeb 29 2012, 06:03 PM
What helped kick off the genre? Well, in a way, all of these games. Or for that matter, let's include the genre's predecessor Dungeons & Dragons as well.Games like DQ1/DW1 and FF1 were early (and successful) attempts at turning this pen-and-paper RPG experience into a videogame. Between them, FF1 actually is a bit closer to the D&D model, what with having a party and character classes, while DQ1 is closer to the modern JRPG (a.k.a. eastern RPG, which is what I think you mean when you say "console RPG"). Other attempts include the Wizardry and Ultima series, from the western side of gaming.Nevertheless, as Doctor Cthulhuforahead puts it, these games were a "strange curiosity" in the west at first. More complex RPGs like Ultima pretty much were there to appeal to the tabletop RPG crowd and maybe draw in some videogamers. That said, in a way, the relatively simplicity of FF1 and DQ1 made the RPG genre more accessible to newcomers.As time went on, the Final Fantasy series became more successful in the west with two of its SNES installments, FF4 and FF6 (FF2 and FF3 as they were called). The technical capabilities of the system (16-bit graphics, a great slate of emulated musical instruments, larger cartridge size etc.) made it increasingly easier to use the RPG to tell an involved, complex story with interesting characters in a custom setting, and this is precisely where the JRPG genre went. FF6 and Chrono Trigger continue to be two of the most critically-acclaimed games for their story and soundtrack.The big break for JRPGs in the west came with Final Fantasy VII, which began using the even greater capabilities of the 32-bit generation (and its CD storage) for storytelling through 3D environments, FMVs, etc.. (Square's heavily promoting the game also helped.)As for western RPGs, I don't know them as well, but as far as I can tell they mostly developed through computer gamers in the west. While consoles tended to be dominated by JRPGs, on computers we see series like Ultima, Diablo, Fable, The Elder Scrolls, Baldur's Gate, Planescape Torment, and the like. WRPGs are making more of a console appearance these days thanks in part to Microsoft being one of the major console manufacturers, but they're still mostly for PCs; compare, say, the catalog of PS2 games and the catalog of games on Steam.
