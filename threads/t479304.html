cgfreak wrote on Jun 07 2013, 10:33 AM:

That's good enough.


First they can't straight up pay gaming sites because a gaming site usually have a lot of people behind it, and you don't know who's gonna go mavrik on you if he leave to work someplace else. Contacting them for the first time to establish this kind of deal is very risky too, publisher wanting to pay for a review score is a huge story and will bring a lot of clicks for the gaming site who bring up such story. So they have to go about manipulating reviews in sneaky ways, here's some.

- The most obvious one and the most used by big publishers is creating close relations with the gaming media. From flying them around the world and paying for their hotel stay while you invite them to your media events, some publishers even did review events, where they bring reviewers to a nice hotel, give them free food/drinks and such for three days, and let them try the game there to write their early reviews, while justifying it as "not wanting the game to leak". Other ways are sending the review copy along with gifts, like figures, free live cards, key chains and suchs. I remember 1up making a video bragging about the stuff that came with Halo 3 review copy, it had a spartan helmet, a Halo themed Xbox 360 and tons of other small stuff, they looked like little babies opening their christmas gifts, not knowing they're documenting themselves while being bribed. Give them enough love and they'd feel bad about reviewing your game badly.
Though, there are gaming sites that strictly oppose this(flown around for free, given gifts, etc) or at least say so publicly.
 
To get a laugh out of how far this can go, here's a letter sent with every Assassin Creed review copy:
 
someone wrote:

It's almost July 4. Time to fire up the grill, invite some friends over and celebrate our nation's birth.

But this July 4 all of us at Ubisoft will have another reason to celebrate. And that's the phenomenal success of our Assassin's Creed brand which couldn't have happened without your incredible support and partnerships. So I'm writing to say, quite simply, 'Thank you.'

Thank you for helping make Assassin's Creed one of the best selling franchises of all time.

Thank you for igniting unprecedented consumer interest in Assassin's Creed 3 which is sure to break plenty of sales records this holiday.

Thank you for helping Assassin's Creed 3 achieve stellar exposure long before launch. Plus a stunning array of honors at E3 that exceeded our wildest expectations. We scored over 40 nominations and took home a bounty of awards including Xplay's Best of Show, Game Informers's Game of Show, and Gamespot's Editor Choice.

So this Independence Day, no matter your nationality, we hope you'll fly this colonial style flag as we celebrate our critical success. And this holiday season, all of us will ignite yet another revolution.

Thanks again,

Tony Key
Sr. VP. Sales and Marketing
Ubisoft Entertainment

 
 
- Gaming sites get most of their income from ads, which are mostly gaming related. So say, Gamespot put a huge ad for Kain and Lynch on their site, but they then publish a bad review of K&L, the publisher would then contact Gamespot parent company and say they want to stop putting ads on Gamespot, GS parent company would then contact Gamespot to change the review, and they would. Look up why Jeff Gerstmann left Gamespot after his K&L review.
Basically, "not biting the hand that feed you" <strike>pure</strike> and simple...

- Exclusive early reviews are always on the positive side, mainly because publisher won't give you an exclusive review rights again if you shat on their game. Marketing people also do research reviewers background to see who might like the game and who might not, ie, who deserve  an early review and who doesn't.
 
- Hiding the shitty parts from the press, the best example being Bethesda not sending PS3 review copies of Skyrim except for "Playstaion only" magazines. This resulted in none of the major review sites reporting how the game is unplayable on PS3, something like this would have brought the game's overall metascore down a lot, if only for the PS3 version.
 
- Using more than one review embargo, "embargo for reviews above 80 ends monday, embargo for all other reviews end on wednesday" for an example, so now if you want to release your review early, it have to be positive, gaming sites can't discuss this openly as they have to sign on a lot of papers to keep getting early review copies. Some journalists mentioned such embargoes though(without mentioning which publisher of course) and you'd notice such things if you pay attention for metascores, where some games would start really high, like in mid 90s, and end up in low 80s after a few days.

- They can plant reviewers(look up Lauren Wainwright), but that usually doesn't end well and won't allow them to control a metascore.

- What Rockstar did before GTAIV's release is very interesting too, it's a collection of a lot of things, some already mentioned. Interesting read if you're interested: http://www.gamasutra.com/php-bin/news_index.php?story=18761#.UI3WM8XA8p0

Those are just some ways in which publishers can influence reviews, it's not as simple as paying for the score they want and they don't have "complete" control over review scores to the point where an AAA game can just straight up pay for a 95 metascore.
