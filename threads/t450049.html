BBob SandersORIGINAL POSTNov 13 2009, 01:06 AM
I have recently been discussing with some friends what the grading system in our schools is. We have come to the conclusion that it is nothing. Allow me to explain:by grading system, I mean the way students are graded (they are given a number on their report card). What does this number mean? Does it mean the student has learned anything? Does it mean the student wants to learn anything? The way I see it, students today are too concerned with getting a good mark rather than actually understanding what they are taught. It just becomes a contest to see who can get the most marks, not who can understand the information the best. There are also many external factors that affect it. The teacher may not like you (this may seem like a poor excuse, but it does affect marks), the teacher's teaching style may not agree with you resulting in you getting a low mark, you may not find the topic interesting, and many others. I once read a study (can't recall from where), but it said that they had 3 groups of students. One group received only marks as feedback, another group received only comments (ie. improve on this, do this more often, etc...) and the third group received both. The students that showed the most growth and improvement and showed that they have the best understanding of the materials taught were the students that received comments only. My teacher also told me of a situation where he decided not to ever tell the students their marks overall (meaning, he would still give them their marks on assignments (and he gave them comments) but he would not tell the students what they had overall in the course). One of the mothers got mad and went to talk with him with her daughter. She asked why her child did not know what her mark was and he responded by telling her that it means nothing and that all students care about is their marks, not how to improve. He then gave an example of an essay. He had given the woman's daughter her essay and the essay had comments on how she could have improved and could improve for the next one. At the meeting, he asked the daughter what mark she got and the girl knew it by heart. He asked the girl what comments he had written as to how she could improve her mark and the girl didn't know a single comment because she did not care to read them. The student was more focused on getting a good mark than on learning how to write better. When a teacher or professor gives you a mark, it does not mean anything. One of my teachers recently gave back one of my marks for a test, but could not tell me which ones I got incorrect so I could read my notes again and understand those things better. What's the point of giving me the mark when I don't know how to improve and how to better understand the things I did not understand? The mark he gave me, essentially, means nothing. Are there ways to improve this system? Is the system fine the way it is? What do you think?

Wow. Several very good posts here.Okay, the identified problem is this: We are not mastering content.This is the key point of all of your posts, and of my own beliefs. Grades are getting in the way of content mastery. Grades should be the measure, not the goal, but for far too many, they are the goal.Ex1: Last semester I took Calculus 1. I failed the first three exams (there are four semester exams and a final). I knew, because of the grading system, that I could not pass the class at that point, so I dropped it. Now, it is possible that I could have really got down and dirty with the subject and figured out and mastered the material by the end of the semester. I could have ended the semester knowing what I needed to know, but having a failing grade and having to retake the class. Because I had to consider the grades for the sake of my G.P.A. which in turn was for the sake of scholarships, I had to drop the class and try again this semester.In this example, the professor used the marks-feedback system. The feedback helped, but the marks hurt more than the feedback helped.I propose this: The only grade that matters is the final grade. In the middle of the semester, you do not fully understand the subject. It is not until you have taken the entire course that you are expected to understand the subject. Throughout the semester you attend the lectures, do the assignments, and receive feedback. At the end of the semester, you are tested over it in some manner. You either get credit, or you don't. There is no A, B, C, F system. You either know it well enough to move on, or you don't. A, or F. This, I believe, puts more focus on content mastery than on the grade. You are no longer fighting for an A over a C, you are only working to master the content. As for those who say this will encourage teachers to teach the test, this is only a problem when the test and curriculum does not cover what actually will be useful for the student in the end. If the curriculum and the test cover the proper material that the student will use in subsequent courses, help the student earn the degree, and eventually help him or her throughout his or her career, then it doesn't matter if the test is being taught. A test is just another learning tool. The content on the test is what matters.Now, to address peoples peopleton's point of very large university classes. I am in several myself: Calculus 1, Physics 1, Sociology, and Intro to Engineering II. Yes, it is impossible for one professor to give feedback on 500+papers on a daily basis. One of my professors still has yet to finish grading our first test because of other aspects of her life that are equally important. This is where TAs come in. TAs are typically graduate students who are several years above that course. If there are enough TAs to go around, the workload on the professor and the TAs is greatly diminished. Although it is still daunting, it is reasonable.

The problem you point out I think can be fixed by this: At my old high school, the GPA was unweighted (4.0 scale) and the class rank was weighted. An A in an AP class gets me a 4.0 and an A in an academic class also gets me an A. Class rank is different: AP classes get an extra 12 points added to the semester grade (only for calculating the rank), honors/dualcredit get 8, academic get 4, and classes such as office aid and remedial classes get no points. This way, a student who makes an 88 average in all of his AP classes will have a higher rank than a student who makes 95s in all of his academic classes.
